#+title: org-truss
#+options: toc:2

Build an argument from members, arrange it into a document, in Org.

It needs [[https://github.com/yoshzucker/org-dock][org-dock]], which takes a
heading out to be written in, and works with
[[https://github.com/yoshzucker/org-moor][org-moor]], for the sources read,
when it is there.

* Why

A truss carries load because its members are joined into triangles.  A
member on its own is just a length of steel; whether it is a chord or a
diagonal depends on where it is placed.  And the same members can be framed
in more than one way -- a Warren truss, a Pratt truss -- for the same span.

A document that argues for something works the same way.  A statement on its
own is only a statement; it becomes the situation, or the grounds, or the
conclusion, by where it is placed.  A claim stands only if something is
under it.  And the same statements can be arranged as fact, interpretation,
action, or as an answer followed by its reasons.

So this package keeps three things apart:

- *members* -- every statement, written once
- *patterns* -- what the members under a heading are to it: its reasons,
  its breakdown, and the order they are told in
- *structure* -- sections that show the members in the order they are
  told

The structure can be rearranged as often as you like without touching a
member, and the board says, at every point, which reasons have no
grounds, which roles a pattern asks for that nothing plays, and what is
taken as given.

Nothing here needs a language model.  The file is meant to be filled by hand;
a model can help fill it, but the structure is yours.

* The model

** Four parts

| Part      | What it holds                                                                            | Where                   |
|-----------+------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+-------------------------|
| Brief     | what the document must satisfy: reader, their question, the response wanted, constraints | ~TRUSS_PART: brief~     |
| Subject   | what was found: premises, issue, constraints, and the facts under them                   | ~TRUSS_PART: subject~   |
| Members   | what is made of it: what the facts mean, and what is to be done                          | ~TRUSS_PART: members~   |
| Structure | a tree of sections, listing the members the document tells                               | ~TRUSS_PART: structure~ |

The brief is the architect's brief: the conditions the design has to meet.
Every check is measured against it.

The subject is the brief's other half.  The brief is about the reader; the
subject is about what the document takes up -- what is taken as given, what
the problem is taken to be, what cannot move.  A proposal is held to both:
the document has to move its reader, and what it proposes has to solve the
problem within its constraints.

Each of the two is broken down as any heading can be.  A new file gives
the brief the breakdown /Reader, Question, Response, Constraints/ and the
subject /Premises, Issue, Constraints/, and a member right under each for
each role, playing it by its ~TRUSS_BREAKDOWN_ROLE~, with a guide to what
to write there.  A role none plays is missing, as anywhere.  Another
breakdown, of your own in ~org-truss-patterns~ or one there, or any other
pattern, can be put on instead: /pattern/ on the part's line on the board puts
it on, and puts in a heading for each role it lacks.

These members are members like any other: they can have grounds, be
assumptions, hold members under them and be shown in the structure.  A
book states its intended reader; a proposal states the constraints it
works within.  Such a statement is written once, under the role it
belongs to, and the structure shows it from there.

The work on a document goes in three steps, one part each.

- /Find out/, in the subject.  Gather the facts, each with its grounds,
  and sum up those that belong together under a member that says in one
  sentence what they say together, and no more.  Its ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ is
  ~reasons~: each fact under it is one of its reasons.
- /Make sense/, in the members.  Say what the facts mean here, and what is
  to be done.  A claim's reasons are nested under it, and stand on the
  facts they link to in their text; what is to be done is broken
  down into steps or parts.
- /Tell/, in the structure.  Sections, each playing a role in how the
  document is told, list the members the reader is shown, from any part.

Where a member is written changes nothing of how it is checked or written
out.  The parts keep what was found apart from what is made of it.

** Patterns and roles

A heading names, in three properties, patterns for the headings right
under it, and each of those says the role it plays in each pattern, in
the pattern's property with ~_ROLE~ after it.

| Pattern property  | Role property          | The headings right under it are | Patterns                                    |
|-------------------+------------------------+---------------------------------+---------------------------------------------|
| ~TRUSS_LOGIC~     | ~TRUSS_LOGIC_ROLE~     | its reasons                     | reasons, deduction, induction               |
| ~TRUSS_BREAKDOWN~ | ~TRUSS_BREAKDOWN_ROLE~ | its breakdown: its parts        | steps, parts, 5W1H, 6W1H, pros and cons ... |
| ~TRUSS_TELLING~   | ~TRUSS_TELLING_ROLE~   | told in the pattern's order     | PREP, SDS, SCQA, Sky, Rain, Umbrella        |

The three are apart.  A heading has at most one of each, and a heading
under it plays a role in each it has: a reason that is also a pro, and
told as PREP's reason, says ~reason~, ~pros~ and ~reason~ in the three.
Nothing is read into a heading that names no pattern: what is under it
is nested there and nothing more, and it asks for nothing.  Every heading
takes all three, and reads them alike: a part, a member, a section.

/Logic/ is the truss itself: what holds the heading up.  Reasons each
hold it up alone; a deduction's major and minor premise hold it up only
together; an induction generalises two cases or more.  A premise that
needs proving is itself the head of a deduction nested under it, so a
chain of reasoning is a tree of them.  Each reason must have grounds;
see [[*Grounds]].

/Breakdown/ is what the heading is made of.  Steps are in order, parts in
none; the other breakdowns name their parts -- pros and cons; past,
present and future; who does it, to whom, when, where, what, why and how
-- so that nothing is left out.  A part needs no grounds of its own: what
holds up the whole holds it up.  A document is written for someone, and
what it asks done is mostly done by someone to someone, so 6W1H is the
cut to reach for when nothing smaller does; 5W1H, without /whom/, is
there for what is done to no one.  A notice reads naturally cut this way,
with its steps inside its /how/:

#+begin_example
Check before every test

- Who: the driver's manager
- Whom: whoever drives in the test
- When: before the first run of the day
- How: as follows

  1. Meet the driver, face to face or by video call
  2. Read the checker
#+end_example

A heading broken down is written out as a list of its parts, steps
numbered, with reasons or without; where the breakdown names its roles,
each part opens with its own, /Who:/, /Pros:/.  So is a section: the
sections below one broken down are items of its list, not headings, each
its title and lead, with what it holds inside.

Steps and parts differ by whether order matters.  /Keep a record of each
check/, in parts: the name of whoever checked, the driver, the time, the
reading, what was said; in any order, nothing is lost.  /Check before
every test/, in steps: meet the driver, then read the checker; in another
order, it is another procedure.

/Telling/ is a framework of telling, which says nothing of what is told:
its places in order, each played by a heading right under the one that
names it.  PREP is a point, a reason, an example and the point again;
each is a heading you write, the point again too, in the words it is to
be told in the second time.

Everything is told in the order of the file: a heading before what is
under it, and what is under it in the order it is written, the order the
reader meets them; the board shows it so.  A telling pattern changes
nothing of that order.  It asks for its places, and what the file has
below a heading the pattern tells after it is marked =⚠= and listed /Out
of order/, to be moved in the file.

So a member's claim is told before its reasons: the pyramid, read top
down.  A story that keeps the conclusion for the end -- SCQA, Sky, Rain,
Umbrella -- is told by the sections of the structure, the claim listed in
the last of them; its reasons come after it there, as they are nested
under it.

| On the board                      | Gives                                                        |
|-----------------------------------+--------------------------------------------------------------|
| logic › reasons                   | reasons that each hold it up alone                           |
| logic › deduction                 | a major and a minor premise, together                        |
| logic › induction                 | two cases or more, that it generalises                       |
| breakdown › steps                 | what to do, in order: a numbered list                        |
| breakdown › parts                 | what it is made of, in no order: a bulleted list             |
| breakdown › What, Why, How        | what it is, why, and how                                     |
| breakdown › Pros and cons         | what is for it, what is against it                           |
| breakdown › Short and long term   | what holds soon, what holds later                            |
| breakdown › Past, present, future | what was, what is, what will be                              |
| breakdown › 5W1H                  | who, when, where, what, why, how                             |
| breakdown › 6W1H                  | who, whom, when, where, what, why, how                       |
| telling › PREP                    | point, reason, example, the point again                      |
| telling › SDS                     | summary, details, the summary again                          |
| telling › SCQA                    | situation, complication, question, answer                    |
| telling › Sky, Rain, Umbrella     | sky, rain, umbrella: what is seen, what it means, what to do |

Sky, Rain, Umbrella is fact, interpretation, action: the sky is clouding
over; it will rain, then; take an umbrella.  It is a way of thinking as
much as of telling, and it tells the umbrella last.

Patterns nest: the umbrella of one Sky, Rain, Umbrella can hold another,
and a deduction can stand where SCQA puts its answer.

/pattern/ on the board offers every pattern, of each kind, on any heading, and
takes one of a kind off.  What the pattern asks for and is not there is
put in, a heading for each role named for it and playing it, where the
pattern lists it, to be written over: a deduction's premises, a PREP's
reason and example, a 6W1H's seven.  On a section, a telling pattern
first takes the sections there into its places by their
~TRUSS_TELLING_ROLE~, and puts them in its order in the file.

Every pattern is written in ~org-truss-patterns~ in one form: its kind,
its roles in order, and, for a breakdown, the list it is written out as.
Each role can say how it is asked for:

| Flag          | The role                                                               |
|---------------+------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| ~:at-least N~ | is asked of N headings or more: induction's cases, two                 |
| ~:any~        | takes what plays no role: SDS's details                                |
| ~:label~      | opens each part playing it with its name when written out: Pros:, Who: |

~org-truss-patterns~ takes patterns of your own in the same form -- a
breakdown from ~org-truss-roles~, a framework of telling -- and
~org-truss-roles~ the roles they name.

** Grounds

A heading that plays a role of the ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ above it is a reason,
and must have grounds -- unless it has a ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ of its own, whose
reasons must have them instead, or is tagged ~:assumption:~, taken as
given.  Whether a heading must have grounds comes from its role alone: not
from the part it is written in, nor from whether anything uses it.

A heading has grounds when its text has one of these:

| a link          | to a member, ~[[#m3]]~, or to a source: a ~moor:~, ~file:~ or URL link |
| ~#+begin_quote~ | the source's own words, quoted                                         |

A link to a member stands on it: a reason in the members on the facts of
the subject it reads (see [[*Pointing to a member]]).  It is grounds on a
line of its own or in a sentence: /as [[#m3]] shows/.  In the text under
a heading the reader sees it too, as the citation it is -- a link to a
member as its words, a ~moor:~ link written out as a link to where its
source came from (see [[*The document]]).  On a comment line, ~# [[#m3]]~,
as /ground/ on the board writes it, it is for the file alone.  So a
source is written once, where it is cited.

Words naming what has no file -- /team agreement/, /what was said in the
hallway/ -- are no grounds: nothing in the file can be gone back to.
Write them in the text, and tag the heading ~:assumption:~: it is then
taken as given, as what no text records is, and the board lists it so.

A link that tells a member, ~[[truss-include:m7]]~, says where the member
is told (see [[*Two links to a heading]]); in a reason it is a ground as
well.  What a link means is what the heading is asked: a reason is asked
why it holds, and a reason that tells the reader m7 and did not stand on
it would put beside itself what has nothing to do with it.  So in a
reason the link both tells m7 and stands on it, on its heading's line or
in its text, and ~[[#m7]]~ stands on m7 without telling it.  On its
heading's line, ~*** [[truss-include:m7]]~, the reason is m7 there, as it
is: a fact that needs no words of yours to be the reason is not said
again by a member of its own.  In a heading that is no reason -- a part
of a breakdown, a section -- the link only tells.

Whether a heading has grounds is read from what it writes, not from what
that points to.  What it points to is asked something of its own, of
every heading alike, whether it must have grounds or not: what is pointed
to as grounds must hold as what it is.

| Pointed to          | Holds when                                                                                                    |
|---------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| a member, ~[[#m3]]~ | it has grounds, or a ~TRUSS_LOGIC~, or the tag ~:assumption:~ -- as a heading that must have grounds is asked |
| a source, ~moor:~   | the truss file's ~sources/~ holds it                                                                          |
| a file, ~file:~     | it is there                                                                                                   |
| a URL               | not checked: nothing in the file can tell                                                                     |

It is asked one step: what a member pointed to points to in turn is asked
of that member, where it is written.  Whether a source says what it is
cited for is not asked of any heading; that is past what the file's
structure can tell.

The line between a fact and an assumption is whether you can say where it
came from.

** One source at a time

The subject is checked against its sources, and a source is read one at a
time, so what brings facts together keeps to one.  Under a
~TRUSS_BREAKDOWN~ of parts, the parts with nothing under them point into
one source; a part with parts under it is held to this itself, and may
point into another -- a form's fields under the form, beside the law
under the law.  A ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ in the subject points into one source in
all it holds.  A source taken by org-moor is one wherever in it its links
point and whatever words they give it, ~[[moor:ID::14]]~ and
~[[moor:ID::200][field B]]~ alike; a file is one, a URL is one.  Links to
members are no sources.

Facts of different sources are brought together by reading them, the
members' work: a reason in the members links to the facts of each (see
[[*Pointing to a member]]).  So the subject reads as its sources do, each
tree checked against one, and the mesh of what stands on what is woven in
the members alone, where each reason shows what it reads.

** What is left to do

Eight checks, each found where the problem is:

| Check         | Found at                       | What it finds                                                                                                                                                     |
|---------------+--------------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Missing roles | the heading whose pattern asks | a role its pattern asks for that no heading right under it plays                                                                                                  |
| No role       | the heading right under it     | plays no role of a pattern of the heading above: its role property is missing, or names none of them                                                              |
| No pattern    | the heading right under it     | says a role in ~TRUSS_LOGIC_ROLE~, ~TRUSS_BREAKDOWN_ROLE~ or ~TRUSS_TELLING_ROLE~ of a kind the heading above has no pattern of; names the patterns with it       |
| Out of order  | the heading right under it     | written below one its parent's ~TRUSS_TELLING~ tells after it                                                                                                     |
| No grounds    | the heading that lacks them    | must have grounds and has none: a link or a quote, a ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ over reasons of its own, or ~:assumption:~ makes it hold                                       |
| Weak grounds  | the heading that points        | points as grounds, in its text, to a member that does not hold                                                                                                    |
| Mixed sources | the heading that brings them   | points into more than one source where it keeps to one: parts with nothing under them, or a ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ in the subject (see [[*One source at a time]])          |
| Broken links  | the link                       | a link to nothing: one that tells or points to no member, one to no source or file; a ~CUSTOM_ID~ two members have; a link that tells what tells its heading back |

They say what is so; what to do about it is written in the file.

* The file

There is one ~truss.org~ per document, in a directory of its own.
~M-x org-truss-init~ sets the directory up; run on one set up before, it
adds what is missing and leaves what is there.

| ~truss.org~ | the manuscript; the rest is written from it or for it                  |
| ~sources/~  | what was read: with org-moor, a directory each, its file and record    |
| ~notes/~    | what is ours and not a source: memos, slides, minutes                  |
| ~attach/~   | what ~org-attach~ (~C-c C-a~) attaches to a heading: a picture, a file |
| ~out/~      | the document, written by ~x~ on the board or ~M-x org-truss-export~    |

So the directory can be moved, or handed over, whole.  With org-moor, a
source taken from a truss file goes in its ~sources/~: a directory named by
the source's id, holding its copy and its record -- a title, where it came
from, how and when.  A member cites it with a ~moor:~ link, ~[[moor:ID::12][Manual
p. 12]]~ for a page or a line, in its text, beside the words it quotes or
on a line of its own; either gives it grounds.  The first line of
~truss.org~ sets ~org-attach-id-dir~ to ~attach/~, so what ~org-attach~ puts
on its headings is kept there, not where attachments go elsewhere, each
heading's in a directory named by its ~ID~; it is a file variable Org
declares safe, so it holds whether org-truss is loaded or not.  The board
and the document read a link to an attachment, ~[[attachment:NAME]]~, as a
link to its file.

~org-truss-project-file~, when it names one, is an empty file written there
too, that marks the directory as a project: ~".project"~, say, with
~project-vc-extra-root-markers~ naming it.  Then the directory is a
project even outside a repository, and what starts at a project's root --
a coding agent among them -- starts beside ~truss.org~ and its sources.
Inside a repository it makes the directory a project apart from the rest.

A comment block at the head of ~truss.org~ holds the rules of the file,
the same for whoever edits it, by hand, on the board, or as a language
model working in the file: what each part holds, what the patterns and
the roles are, which headings must have grounds and what grounds are, how
one member points to another, what is written out, what each other
property and tag means, what goes in the directories beside it, and the
command that checks the file.  A folded ~:GUIDE:~ drawer under each part,
and under each member playing a role of the brief's or the subject's
breakdown, says how to work there.  The board's keys are at its foot, and
what each check finds is said where it is reported.  The headings of a new
file are English whatever ~org-truss-language~ says: they name the parts
and the roles, and the reader never sees them.

The guidance learns, and a file keeps the wording it was written with.
~M-x org-truss-refresh-guides~ puts the current wording back -- the comment
block and every drawer, adding those that were deleted -- and touches
nothing else you wrote.

** Headings assert; the structure tells

A member's heading is the sentence it asserts, and what the checks read.
Links point to it by its ~CUSTOM_ID~, not its heading, so the heading can
be reworded at any time.  It can be wrong and can stand on others,
so write it to read on its own, pulled out of the document -- no "the
following", no "above", no "first".  The order it is told in is the
structure's, and a heading that leans on that order stops being true when
the structure changes.  What a heading asserts is tied to the reader's
question, in the brief: ask another question and the top claim changes,
while what it stood on stays.  It is not tied to how it is told.

The structure asserts nothing.  It chooses what the reader sees and in
what order, and writes only what guides them -- what is true of the
document rather than of the world, so it needs no grounds:

- a section's title, its heading;
- a section's lead, the text under its heading, read under the title
  before the members the section tells.  The structure's own lead opens
  the document;
- the links that tell members, where they stand -- in its items, or in
  its lead, between the words that lead from one to the next.

The joins between members are seldom the structure's to write either: a
member's patterns say what is under it is to it -- steps numbered, a part

be wrong is a member, where the checks reach it.

A member is told by its heading, wherever it is told, and the text under
the heading follows it: what it says more to the reader.  To say it to
the reader otherwise, rewrite the heading -- what it asserts is what the
reader is told.  What is for you alone -- why you believe it, who said
it, what cannot be said -- goes in a comment, as Org writes one, which
stays in the file:

A member tagged ~:noexport:~ stays in
the file whole, with what is nested under it: evidence you keep for
yourself, a note on what to check.  A document generated from the file can
be regenerated as often as you change it, so the file stays the place where
the thinking happens, and the document is never a copy that has to be kept
in step by hand.

** What the outline means

Each part is found by its ~TRUSS_PART~, at whatever level and in whatever
order the parts sit, so the file can be laid out as suits you.  Within the
parts, the outline carries meaning in these places only:

- Under any heading with a pattern, the headings right under it play its
  roles, by their role properties, and the order they are written in is
  the order a telling pattern tells them in, and steps are done in.
- Under the /Brief/, the /Subject/ and the /Members/, a heading is a
  member, and the order of the members there means nothing besides; the
  board and the document follow the links.
- Under /Structure/, the outline is the document's: its sections, in order.

So members can be reordered and their headings rewritten freely; nesting
one under another, though, makes it play a role in the other's patterns,
and a member moved under a section would be read as a section.  The board
does not depend on where in the file a member is written -- only on what
it is nested under and what links to it.

** Two links to a heading

A heading -- a member or a section -- is linked to in one of two ways,
and the kind of link alone says which: not where it is written, nor
whether it is a list item, nor what kind of heading it points to.

| Link                   | What it does                                                                            |
|------------------------+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| ~[[#m7]]~              | points to heading m7: a ground, in a heading's text                                     |
| ~[[truss-include:m7]]~ | tells heading m7 there: it, with what is under it, is written out where the link stands |

A link that tells a heading can stand in any heading -- a section, or a
member that brings another along -- on a line of its own, as a list item,
or in a sentence; or on a section's
heading line, ~*** [[truss-include:m7]]~.  Where it stands says how it is
told.  On a heading line, it is a heading of the document: its words,
then what is under it, under headings a level down.  In text, it is its
words in the sentence, and the rest -- its body, then what is under it --
follows the paragraph, or goes inside the list item, as a list.  Its
description, ~[[truss-include:m7][words]]~, is its words there.  A
section told is told as a member is.
Two places that tell the same section tell its chapter twice: that is
yours to want or not.  A link that points to a heading reads as its
description, or the heading; in a comment it is not told.  What a heading
brings with it is told inside it, and so on down; a link that would tell
what tells it back, without end, is reported under /Broken links/, and
the document says so where it stops.

A section tells the members it shows by such links, ~- [[truss-include:m7]]~,
from whatever part they are written in.  A section can hold sections of
its own.

** Members as items of a list

A member whose reasons, parts or steps say little beyond their one line
need not have a heading each: written as the items of a list in its
text, marked ~#+attr_truss~, they are members right under it all the
same -- the patterns ask for them, the checks reach them, the board draws
them, and links point to them.

#+begin_src org
,** We move to fortnightly reviews
We would meet half as often.
,#+attr_truss: :logic reasons :breakdown pros-cons
- pros :: <<m21>>Less time in meetings
  # [[#m2]]
  An hour a fortnight back.
- cons :: Items wait longer
  ,#+attr_truss: :breakdown steps
  1. Ask the leads
  2. Ask the team
#+end_src

- ~#+attr_truss~ names patterns as the properties do, ~:logic~,
  ~:breakdown~ and ~:telling~; the member takes them for the kinds its
  properties leave out.  Its items come before the headings under it.
- The term before ~::~ is the role an item plays, by its English name, as
  in ~TRUSS_BREAKDOWN_ROLE~.  A pattern of one role -- ~reasons~, ~steps~,
  ~parts~ -- is played by every item, with a term or none; a term that
  names no role is part of the item's words.
- An item's first line is its words, as a heading's are; the lines under
  it are its text, a comment left out; a list marked so inside it holds
  the members under it.
- A target in its first line, ~<<m21>>~, is its id: ~[[m21]]~ points to
  it, as a ground, and ~[[truss-include:m21]]~ tells it.  /place/ and
  /ground/ give an item one, as they give a heading a ~CUSTOM_ID~.
- In the document the items are told as the headings under a member are,
  after its text: each opened with its role where the pattern names it,
  the list's marks and the targets left out.

On the board, /edit/ on an item takes out the heading whose text holds
it, at the item; the commands that change a heading -- /rename/, /refile/,
/delete/, /pattern/ and the rest -- say so on an item instead, which is
written by hand there.

** Pointing to a member

Both links point to a heading by its ~CUSTOM_ID~: ~m7~ is the heading
with ~:CUSTOM_ID: m7~; an item of a list, by its target, ~<<m7>>~, with
~[[m7]]~.  Org resolves ~[[#m7]]~ within the file alone -- ~C-c
C-o~ follows it, in a section or in a property -- and, unlike ~:ID:~, it
is not entered in ~org-id-locations~, nor made a node by org-roam; with
org-truss loaded, ~C-c C-o~ follows ~[[truss-include:m7]]~ the same way.

A heading is what a member asserts, and it gets reworded as the argument
and the document take shape.  Were links to name headings, each rewording
would break what pointed to it; pointing by id, a heading can be rewritten
anywhere -- on the board with /rename/, in the file by hand, or by a model
editing the file -- and nothing breaks.

A member is given an id only once something points to it: /place/ placing it,
/ground/ making it a ground, /new/ writing it in a section.  An id is ~m~ and the
next number free in the file; one you write yourself is kept.  Two
headings with one id, and a link or a ground that points to no heading,
are reported as /Broken links/, at each in the part it is in.  /place/ on a broken link places a member where it
stands.

A link written by heading, ~[[*heading]]~ or ~[[truss-include:*heading]]~
-- with Org's completion, say -- points to no member and shows as broken.
~M-x org-truss-convert-references~ points each by id, wherever it is,
giving the member named one, and keeps a link's words.

A file written while grounds were a property, ~TRUSS_GROUNDS~, is read
without them; ~M-x org-truss-convert-grounds~ moves each into the text of
its heading -- each link on a line of its own after the heading's text,
the words after them -- and tags ~:assumption:~ a heading the words alone
grounded.

A file written while only a public block, ~#+begin_public~, was told,
and the rest of a heading's text stayed in the file, is told now as it
stands: ~M-x org-truss-convert-public~ puts what each heading had outside
its public block into a comment block, makes the block's text the
heading's text, and keeps a title other than the heading on a comment
line above it, ~# Title for the reader: ...~, for the heading to be
rewritten by hand.  A file with no public block is left as it is.  All
three leave the buffer unsaved, to be looked over.

* The board

~M-x org-truss-board~ draws the nearest ~truss.org~: under its title, its
brief, its subject, its members and its structure, in the order the work
is done, each followed by what is left to do there.

The board is where a truss file is worked from, as the agenda is for Org
files.

A heading of any other Org file can be drawn on a board of its own: in a
file that is no truss file, ~M-x org-truss-board~ draws the nearest heading
at or above point with a pattern -- or, when no truss file is near, the
heading at point -- as a truss file's members are drawn, under a line
/Heading/.  Its patterns, the headings under it by the roles they play,
and the roles still missing are all there, without a brief, a subject or a
structure.  /pattern/ on it puts a pattern on it, and a heading in for each
role, to be written over -- and in the file itself, in a truss file or any
other, ~M-x org-truss-apply-pattern~ does what /pattern/ does, on the heading at
point; written over with the statement that plays it, a role still shows
on the right what it is -- /Who:/, /When:/ -- so the heading need not say
it.  An agent asked about it with org-dock is told what its patterns ask,
and how to check its copy; see [[*Batch][Batch]].
It can be called from anywhere: when no ~truss.org~ is near the current
buffer, or with a prefix argument, it asks for one as ~find-file~ asks for
a file -- the file, its directory, or anything below it -- and offers to
start one in a directory that has none, making the directory if it is
new.  The file's own buffer can be closed whenever it is not wanted: the
board visits it again before its next command.  ~C-x C-s~, or whatever
saves a buffer, saves it from the board, and the title line says
/unsaved/ until then.

Either way ~TAB~ opens a member where it is one line.

Each line has three columns.  On the left is what the board says the line
is: the role a section or a member plays in the patterns above
it -- /member/ for one that plays none -- and the tree that joins them.
After the role come, in brackets, what wants seeing to: =[no role]= for a
heading that plays no role of a pattern above it, =[no pattern]= for one
that plays a role the heading above has no pattern for, =[assumed]= for an
assumption, =[no grounds]= for a reason that must have grounds and has
none.  At the end of the left, just before its heading, a member is named
by its ~CUSTOM_ID~ as the file names it: =#m9= where it is written, in the
subject and the members, and =[[#m9]]= where a link that tells it or a
ground calls it.  So what the structure shows can be found where it is
written, and a member nested under one a link calls, which is not called
itself, has none.  In the middle, from one column the same on every line,
are the file's own words -- headings, labels, the title, the brief -- as
written, with dots leading to them.  A member's heading is in the face Org
gives a heading of its level, so it looks as it does in the file; the
board colours it itself, so it does not matter whether the file has been
shown yet.  The headings of the parts are not among them: the
structure's, like the brief's and the members', names a part and never
reaches the reader, so its line says only /Structure/.  Words too long for
the middle are cut short, the whole of them in the tooltip, so the right
keeps its column.  The left is as wide as it needs; the middle and the
right share the rest of the window evenly, and are drawn again when it is
resized.  What a section or a member holds starts two columns right of
its word: what it stands on, the members its links tell.  The
sections under a section, and the members nested under a member, branch
off from the column of its word.  The line of the tree starts right under
the line naming its patterns and runs down past whatever comes before the
first branch -- what it stands on or the members its links tell, and with entry
text its comments -- so each heading is held by it as far down as it goes.
The members' tree is drawn in the face ~org-truss-branch~, faint by
default, so where it meets the sections' tree in the structure the two
read apart.

The three columns say the same in every part.  The left is where a line
stands in the patterns; the middle, what is asserted, so whether the logic
holds -- or, in the structure, whether the story does -- is read down it;
the right, what the reader is told, so whether it reads as prose is read
down that.  A member's right is the same where it is written and where a
link tells it: its heading, and with entry text the text under it; a step with its number,
a part with its role.  It is in the face ~org-truss-untold~ while the
document does not tell it.  So a heading and what is under it can be read through as
prose beside its logic where it is written.

There are two kinds of link, and the board draws each at both its ends.
A ground is written by the heading that stands on it, ~[[#m12]]~, in its
text: logic, never told.  A link that tells a
member, ~[[truss-include:m12]]~, is written by the heading that tells it,
a section or a member: what the reader is shown, and where.  Every line drawn for an end of a link is marked, and a line
with no mark is written where it stands, as a heading in the file.  The
mark says what what the line names is to the one above it, the member or
the section the line hangs from:

|------+----------------------------------------+--------------------------------------------------------|

|      | source, or the words it quotes         |                                                        |

=⏚= and =•= are where a link is written, and name what it calls by its
id; =↥= and =▤= are the other end, where a member is written.  A line at
the end where a link is written says what the link is to its heading --
=⏚ ground=, =• told= -- not the role the member plays where it is
written, which says nothing there; a line at the other end names the
heading the link is in by the role that heading plays.  A heading whose
words are a link, ~*** [[truss-include:m7]]~, shows only the link in the
middle, so the part m7 is written in is said before it, where an id
goes: =→Subject=, =→Members=, =→Brief=, =→Structure= -- how near to its
sources what it stands on is.  What a
member stands on is drawn below it, as the ground is below a building:
=⏚ ground= a member it points to -- with =[no grounds]= when it does not
hold -- then =⏚ quote= each quotation in its text, and below them
=⏚ source= each source it links to, with =[not found]= when the link
points to nothing: the words are read from the source, so the source is
furthest down.  A quotation, and a line of its text holding nothing but
such a link, is not drawn again in its text, and a link in a sentence
there is drawn as it is written, =[[#m3]]= or =[[moor:6f1c::12][Manual
p. 12]]=, the words it points to left to the line below.  What stands on a
member is drawn below it too, as everything under a line is, so its mark
points up, from the member to it.  A line marked =⏚=, =↥= or
=▤= is cut to the middle, its whole heading in the tooltip; the mouse on
any mark says what it means, and ~RET~ on the line goes to what it names.
/same/ goes between a member and its calls.  A member with no line marked =▤=
or =↥= is not used.  ~org-truss-board-marks~ sets the marks.  A role a
member's pattern asks for and it lacks -- /Minor premise —/ -- follows what
it holds, with the pattern that asks when the member has more than one.
So does a role the structure's or a section's pattern asks for and no
section right below plays -- /Rain —/ -- and /new-section/ on it adds a section that
plays it.

In the structure a member a link tells is one line, as written, the
words it is told in on its right; opened there, as told (/order/), it brings
with it what the document tells -- what is nested under it -- beside its
uses, what it stands on and its gaps, so the document can be read down
the right.

Each part is followed by what is left to do in it, check by check --
/Broken links/, /No grounds/, /Weak grounds/, /Mixed sources/, /Missing
roles/, /No role/, /No pattern/, /Out of order/
(see [[*What is left to do]]) -- each thing listed in the part it is found
in, and ~RET~ on it goes there.  The mouse on the name of a check says
what it finds.

The patterns a heading names go on the line just below it, in
parentheses, where the headings they arrange branch off.  A step or a
part shows on the right the number, the bullet or the label the reader
will see it with, and a reason the label of the role it plays in a
breakdown.  A section marked =⚠= comes below a section the pattern tells
after it; the document gives sections in the order of the file, so the
order is put right there.  The mark's tooltip, or ~C-h .~ on the line,
says which.

What the reader sees is the words the section tells a member in, when it
has some; else its heading -- for a section, its heading in the
document; /noexport/ when the reader sees nothing of it.  On the
/Structure/ line is the start of the structure's own lead.  A heading the
reader sees is drawn as a title; the text under it, in the faint face the
comments in the middle take.  The brief is the reader's only through the title.
Under the title come the brief, then the subject, each drawn as the
members are: its line, its patterns, and the members under it, each named
by the role it plays, so what the structure has to meet stays in view.  A
role the breakdown asks for and none plays -- /Constraints —/ -- follows
them, and /new/ on it writes a member playing it.  A member that plays none
of the breakdown's roles is marked =[no role]=, and /roles/ gives it one.

/entry-text/, as in the agenda, shows what is written under each heading, below its
line: the comments that stay in the file in the middle, under the heading,
and the text the reader sees on the right, broken to fit the window.
A heading too long for the middle is broken there too instead of cut
short, since the board is now for reading, and so is every other line: a
member's text, a ground, what a check lists.  The line naming a heading's
patterns stays right under its line, on the left of what follows it; when
it is too wide for the left it takes the line after the heading's words,
and the comments start below it.  Read down the right, with the members
opened where told, the board is then the document, near enough, beside
the tree and the comments it was built from:

~i~ does the same for the line at point alone, inline as ~i~ in Dired
shows a directory, so the board can be scanned a line each and one line
read through: a member with its text, a ground.  ~i~ again, on
any of its lines, puts it back on one.  While /entry-text/ shows every line whole,
~i~ puts the line at point on one line instead, and /entry-text/ again draws every
line the same way.

A picture linked on a line of its own -- an attachment, or a file by a
relative path -- is drawn on a line drawn whole, in its column: as wide as
the column and no taller than ~org-truss-board-image-height~ lines, on the
lines it takes, so the tree beside it runs on down and the reader's column
stays where it is.  On one line, and where Emacs shows no pictures, a link
to a file reads as the file's name.

The board's words are English whatever ~org-truss-language~ says, so in a
file written in another language the two sides read apart at a glance.

The board is read-only.  Its commands change the truss file and draw the
board again.  The writing itself happens in the file: ~RET~, ~o~ and /show/
open it where the line points, as RET, TAB and SPC do in the agenda, and /edit/ takes just
that heading out, by org-dock, into a copy of its own beside the board, with
what is under it and the rest of the file out of the way.  The headings
under it are folded to their lines, and its own text is shown with its
blocks open -- a comment block, its quotation -- even where
~org-hide-block-startup~ folds blocks, since they are part of what it
says.  While it is out the heading cannot change in the file.  As in a
capture buffer, ~C-c C-c~ there writes it back, saves the file and closes
the copy, and ~C-c C-k~ lets the copy go, the heading as it was; its header
line says so.  ~C-c C-c~ is taken for this, so a source block in an entry
is run with ~C-c C-v e~ instead.  An agent can be asked to work on the
copy, and on nothing else; see org-dock.
Back on the board after the file has changed, it draws again, and before
each command it draws again first if the file's text has changed, so the
command acts on the file as it is.  Only the text counts: fontifying the
file, which happens whenever it is shown, changes nothing the board holds.
An edit made while a command is asking something makes the command stop
and draw again rather than act on positions that have moved.

The board's commands fall into four groups, by what they work on.
/Write/ works on the members themselves, the headings in the file.
/Place/ works on the links that tell them: a section tells the members it
shows, and the members do not move.  /Shape/ works on the sections, on
what the structure says itself, and on the patterns of any heading.
/See/ moves about the board and the document.  Each is named here by what
follows ~org-truss-board-~ in its name -- /place/ is
~org-truss-board-place~.  Few have a key of their own: those that move
through the board and read it, ~m~ for a source, ~x~ for the document.
The rest are called by name, ~M-x org-truss-board-place~, or bound where
you want them, in ~org-truss-board-mode-map~.  The foot of the board lists
those with a key, in the same four lines.

/place/ on any heading -- a section, a member, a part -- writes in it a link
that tells the heading chosen, ~- [[truss-include:m7]]~: a member, those
placed nowhere offered first, or a section.  A heading can be told in as
many places as want it, and is told in each; /take-out/ takes it out of one.
On a broken link, /place/ tells the heading chosen where the link stands, in
its place: the way to mend one.  /take-out/ loses nothing
written: on a member a link tells, the link goes and the member stays in
the file, whatever heading the link is in; a section taken out hands what
it tells up, asking before its own text is lost.  To be rid of
a member, /delete/ deletes it, with what is under it and the lines that tell
it; a ground that named it is left, a broken link on the board, so what
stood on it shows.

/say/ writes the text the reader is told under a heading: it opens the
heading beside the board as /edit/ opens an entry, at an empty line made
before the first link that tells a member -- a section's lead -- or after
the heading's text when no link tells one; the line goes again when the
entry is let go.  On the /Structure/ line, it writes the document's
opening.

/same/ goes between where a heading is written and where it is called, a
member or a section.  On a line calling it -- a link that tells it,
=[[#m9]]=, or a ground -- or showing it under a member a link calls, it
goes to where it is written, =#m9=, unfolding what hides it.  There, it goes to the next call after the one
come from, and round, saying which of how many it is.  The line at point
is lit, and so are the other lines naming the member on it -- where it is
written, where it is called -- in the face ~org-truss-same~; set
~org-truss-board-highlight~ to nil for neither.

~TAB~ cycles a line as it cycles a heading in Org.  A folded line opens
to what is right under it, each of those folded; again, everything under
it opens; again, it folds into its line, which then says how many it
hides.  Under a member, that is what is nested under it and what its
links tell; under a section, the sections below it and what its links
tell.  On a line with nothing under it, the line it is under cycles.
~C-u TAB~, or ~S-TAB~, opens every line while any is folded, and else
folds them all.  /fold/ folds one line or unfolds it, without the cycle.
The board starts as an Org file does in overview, and as ~C-u TAB~ folds
it: every line with something under it folded, each line on one line.
~org-truss-board-fold-limit~, 0 unless set, is how many a heading may hold
and still start open; nil starts the board open.  A member a link tells
starts folded where it is not opened, as /order/ says, since it is drawn
whole where it is.

Folding and drawing a line whole are apart: ~TAB~ shapes the tree, ~i~
reads one line through, and each leaves the other as it is.  Each line
starts on one line; ~C-u i~ draws every line whole, as /entry-text/ does,
and again every line on one.

A link is drawn as what it is to its heading.  In a reason every link
to a member is a ground, so each is a =⏚ ground= under it, in m7's words
and called by its id, =[[#m7]]=, as any ground is.  None is drawn there
besides as a member told.  A reason whose heading's line is the link,
~*** [[truss-include:m7]]~, is drawn as written, its role and the link,
which says already that it tells m7.  Under m7, where it is written, each
reason is one line, =↥=, built on it.  In a heading that is no reason
the link only tells, and is drawn as =• told=.

A heading keeps the words of its source with it, in a ~#+begin_quote~
block in its own text, which is written out as the quotation it is -- in
a comment block it stays in the file -- and gives it
grounds; it may keep several, each drawn as a =⏚ quote= line of its own.
/edit/ on the heading, or on a quotation's line, writes one or rewrites it.
With org-moor, reading a source beside it -- ~org-moor-noter~ -- writes
the quotation and the link to its page or line in one go.

~m~, with org-moor, takes a source for the heading as org-moor takes one,
into the truss file's ~sources/~, and writes the link to it on a line of
its own after the heading's text, the reader's citation too: ~m w~ a page
or a file on the web, its copy fetched in the background
(~org-moor-take-url~); ~m f~ a file (~org-moor-take-file~); ~m c~ the
clipboard (~org-moor-take-clipboard~).  ~m l~ links a source the file
has, by its title, and ~m k~ keeps a copy, from their origins, of the
sources the heading links to that have none -- those an agent took
without being able to fetch them as you, signed in (~org-moor-keep-copy~).
~m o~ opens a source to check what a heading says against it: on a
source's own line, the one marked =⏚=, that source; on a quotation's, the
source it quotes, linked in it or else the first the text cites; on a
heading's, one its own text links to, asked for when there are several.
The copy, at the page or line the link names, as following the link
does; ~m O~ where it came from instead, the page on the web.  A link to
anything but a source org-moor took is followed as it is, with either.
A link put on a comment line, ~# [[moor:...]]~, is the file's alone.
/ground/ makes a heading -- a member or a section -- something it stands
on, a link to it on a comment line of its own after the heading's text,
~# [[#m3]]~, which the reader is not told: it offers what
it stands on first, and choosing one takes that line away; any other is
added, and given a ~CUSTOM_ID~ when it has none.  On a ground's own line, the one marked =⏚=, /ground/ takes that ground
away at once; one linked in the text is edited there, with /edit/.  /assume/ tags
a heading ~:assumption:~, or takes the tag off.  Each works on a section
as on a member: a section has grounds as any heading does.

/pattern/ works the same on every line with a heading: on the /Structure/ line
it gives the document a pattern -- a telling pattern, Sky, Rain,
Umbrella, SCQA, PREP, with its sections -- and on a section the same for
the sections below it; on the /Brief/ or the /Subject/ line, it changes
what they are broken down into, and puts in a member for each role none
plays.  ~no logic~, ~no breakdown~ and ~no telling~ take each off, and
nothing is asked of what is below for it.

The foot of the board lists the commands with a key, as they are bound
where it is drawn, so a key moved or added -- by an Evil configuration,
say -- shows as it is, and a command with none is left off.

** Writing members from the board

A statement can be started without deciding where it goes.  /new/ asks for
its heading, writes it, and opens it as /edit/ does, below the heading, to be
written; ~C-c C-k~ there takes it away again.  It goes under the heading
the line at point is:

- a member, or the brief, the subject or the members; on a missing role,
  playing that role, where the pattern lists it;
- on a section, at the end of the members, and told in the section by a
  link;
- anywhere else, at the end of the members, where order means nothing.

Under a heading with patterns, the new member plays a role in each: the
one a pattern has alone -- a reason under reasons, a step under steps --
without asking, and else the one asked for, those no heading plays yet
first.  It goes where the patterns list the roles it plays: before the
first heading there that plays one listed after its own, or else last --
as /pattern/ puts in what a pattern asks for, and /new-section/ a section.

/refile/ refiles a heading, as Org's ~C-c C-w~ does: it moves it, with what is
under it, last under another -- a member under the brief, the subject, the
members, or another member; a section under another section, or the
structure.  Links and grounds point to a member by its ~CUSTOM_ID~, which
goes with it, so the structure still shows it wherever it goes.  /up/ and
/down/ move a heading among its siblings in the file, and a member a link
tells among the links there: what the file has in an order the
telling pattern does not want, listed /Out of order/, is put right from
the board.  The places are offered in the order of the file, and a member
by its path -- from its part, through what it is nested under, cut short,
to its own heading, with a step's number or the roles it plays: /Members ›
Check before every … › As follows › step 2: Read the checker/.  /place/ on a
section offers members the same way.

Applying a telling pattern to a section that already has sections puts
each, as it is, in the place of the new section whose role it plays; the
pattern adds sections, named in ~org-truss-language~, only for the places
none plays.  So switching patterns changes the order and the marks, never
a heading.  A section in none of the places is kept after the rest, and
the board marks it =[no role]=.  /place/, /roles/ and /take-out/ sort it out.  /unwrap/ and /take-out/
ask before discarding text.  /unwrap/ takes the section's patterns
off too, of every kind, since what they asked for was asked of the
sections it takes away.

* The document

/view/ on the board shows the document in another window without writing it,
and the board keeps it current while it is shown: the place to read it.
The right of the board comes close but is not the same.  The brief and
the subject under the title and the lists of what is left to do are not
in the document, and without /entry-text/ each line shows only a title or the
start of a paragraph.

~M-x org-truss-export~, or ~x~ on the board, walks the structure and writes
what the reader sees to ~out/truss.org~ (~org-truss-output-directory~), then runs
pandoc, when it is installed, to make ~out/truss.md~.

- Under every heading, what is written out is its text, a comment left
  out: a line starting with ~#~ and a space, or a ~#+begin_comment~ block.
- The document is titled by the file's ~#+title~; the structure's text,
  its lead, opens the document.
- A section is titled by its heading; its text, its lead, follows the
  title.  One tagged ~:noexport:~ is left out, with what is under it, as
  a member is.
- A link that tells a member, ~[[truss-include:m7]]~, is the member
  written where it stands, in its words: its link's description, else its
  heading.  What follows its words is its text, the links in it telling
  what they tell where they stand; then what is nested under it, in the
  order of the file.  One tagged ~:noexport:~ is left out, with what is
  nested under it.  A link that points to one, ~[[#m7]]~, is written as
  its description, or the member's heading.
- Where the link stands says the shape.  On a section's heading line,
  ~*** [[truss-include:m7]]~, the member's words are that heading, and
  what is nested under it is told under headings a level down, as the
  sections are.  In text -- a section's lead, a list item, a sentence --
  its words are written in place and the rest follows the paragraph, or
  goes inside the list item, as a list one level in.  Once a list, what
  is nested stays a list.  Below the sixth level,
  Markdown's last, what is under a heading is a list.
- What a heading with a logic or a breakdown holds -- its reasons, its
  parts -- is the content of what it says, and is written as a list, the
  heading itself a heading still: so a heading of the document is a
  claim, and the list below it what holds it up.  A telling says only
  the order, and what is under it is told as the heading is.
- A heading broken down, member or section, has its parts written as a
  list, with reasons or without: steps numbered, the rest bulleted, each
  part opening with its role where the breakdown names its roles.  What
  is nested under a part goes inside its item, as a list again; a
  section's title and lead open its item, and the members it lists and
  the sections below it follow inside.
- A link that tells no member is written as /(broken link: …)/, and one
  that would tell without end as /(told without end: …)/, where it will
  be seen.
- A link to a file by a relative path -- a picture in ~attach/~, a source --
  is written relative to ~out/~, so the document still finds it, and pandoc
  makes a picture an image.  /view/ shows the pictures where they are.
- A ~moor:~ link, with org-moor, goes where its source came from, since
  the copy in ~sources/~ is yours and the reader has none: to the page on
  the web, at the page the link names when it is a PDF,
  ~[[https://e.x/act.pdf#page=14][Act p. 14]]~.  A source that came from
  no such page -- a file, a mail -- is named by the link's words, or its
  title, as text.  /view/ keeps the link to the copy, to be followed in Emacs.

pandoc is given ~org+east_asian_line_breaks~, so a filled paragraph of
Chinese, Japanese or Korean does not gain a space at every line break.
~org-truss-pandoc-arguments~ and ~org-truss-output-extension~ change the
target; pandoc's ~--reference-doc~ gives a slide or word-processor template.

* Batch

#+begin_src sh
emacs --batch -L . -L ../org-dock -l org-truss -f org-truss-batch-check  truss.org
emacs --batch -L . -L ../org-dock -l org-truss -f org-truss-batch-board  truss.org
emacs --batch -L . -L ../org-dock -l org-truss -f org-truss-batch-export truss.org
emacs --batch -L . -L ../org-dock -L ../org-moor -l org-truss -l org-moor \
      -f org-truss-batch-take truss.org FILE-OR-URL [TITLE]
#+end_src

The first prints what is left to do, as ~M-x org-truss-check~ lists it:
under the name of each check that finds something and what it finds, a
line for each thing, ~truss.org:42: …~.  Anything that edits the file
without the board -- you by hand, or a model working in the file -- can
run it to see what it broke or left undone.  The comment block at the head
of a truss file gives this line, with the directories org-truss and
org-dock are loaded from, and org-moor, when it is there, so that ~moor:~
links are checked too.

Any other Org file is checked as the board of one heading reads it: each
heading with a pattern and none above it with one.  A copy taken out by
org-dock is checked written back where it came from, so that its links,
grounds and sources are found as they will be: what is in the copy is
given by the copy's lines, and what is outside it only when the copy
brings it about.  An agent handed a copy is told to check it so, with its
patterns, what its heading plays in the pattern above it, and -- out of a
truss file -- that the rest of the rules are in the file's head comment;
the same words whichever heading, or the whole file, was taken out.  The second prints the board as text, for
anything that reads the structure without Emacs open.

The last, with org-moor, takes a file -- copied, never moved -- or a web
page into the truss file's ~sources/~ and prints a link to it named by its
title, for a model working in the file, which cannot ask Emacs: written
in a member's text, it cites the source.  The comment block gives this line
too when org-moor is there.
