#+title: org-truss
#+options: toc:2

Build an argument from members, arrange it into a document, in Org.

* Why

A truss carries load because its members are joined into triangles.  A
member on its own is just a length of steel; whether it is a chord or a
diagonal depends on where it is placed.  And the same members can be framed
in more than one way -- a Warren truss, a Pratt truss -- for the same span.

A document that argues for something works the same way.  A statement on its
own is only a statement; it becomes the situation, or the grounds, or the
conclusion, by where it is placed.  A claim stands only if something is
under it.  And the same statements can be arranged as fact, interpretation,
action, or as an answer followed by its reasons.

So this package keeps the three apart:

- *members* -- every statement, written once
- *arguments* -- members joined, so that a claim rests on what supports it
- *structure* -- sections that show those arguments in the order they are
  told

The structure can be rearranged as often as you like without touching a
member, and the board says, at every point, which claims stand on nothing,
which questions the reader will ask that the document does not answer, and
what is being assumed.

Nothing here needs a language model.  The file is meant to be filled by hand;
a model can help fill it, but the structure is yours.

* The model

** Five parts, and a sixth that is not a layer

| Part      | What it holds                                                                            | Where                     |
|-----------+------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+---------------------------|
| Brief     | what the document must satisfy: reader, their question, the response wanted, constraints | ~TRUSS_PART: brief~       |
| Subject   | what was found: premises, issue, constraints -- facts, summed up in clusters             | ~TRUSS_PART: subject~     |
| Members   | what is made of it: interpretations and decisions, each written once                     | ~TRUSS_PART: members~     |
| Clusters  | a heading and what is right under it: a claim and its support, a section and its own     | any heading with headings |
|           | sections, the brief or the subject and its fields; the structure is the largest          | under it                  |
| Structure | a tree of sections, each a place in how the document is told                             | ~TRUSS_PART: structure~   |
| Patterns  | three kinds, each a property of its own, for any cluster: logic, aspects, telling        | ~org-truss-patterns~      |

The brief is the architect's brief: the conditions the design has to meet.
Every check is measured against it.

The subject is the brief's other half.  The brief is about the reader; the
subject is about what the document takes up -- what is taken as given, what
the problem is taken to be, what cannot move.  A proposal is held to both:
the document has to move its reader, and what it proposes has to solve the
problem within its constraints.

The brief and the subject are clusters, as a claim with its support is.
Each has an aspects pattern, which a new file writes --
/Reader, Question, Response, Constraints/ on the brief, /Premises, Issue,
Constraints/ on the subject -- and its fields are the headings right under
it, each filling one of the pattern's places by its ~TRUSS_ROLE~.  A
place no field fills is a gap, as a place no section fills is in the
structure.  Another pattern, of your own in ~org-truss-patterns~ or one
of the aspects there, gives a part other fields: ~p~ on its line on the
board puts it on, and puts in a field for each place it lacks.

What is written under a field stays in the file, as notes do.  A field can
also hold statements, as headings under it, and those are members like any
other: they have a source, can be assumptions or questions, and can be
shown in the structure.  A book states its intended reader; a proposal
states the constraints it works within.  Such a statement is written once,
in the field it fills, and the structure shows it from there.

** Three steps: find out, make sense, tell

The work on a document goes in three steps, one part each.

- /Find out/, in the subject.  Gather the facts, each with its source, and
  sum them up in clusters: the head of a cluster says in one sentence what
  the facts nested under it say together, and no more.  Its ~TRUSS_LOGIC~
  says that what is under it is its breakdown -- ~how › parts~, which ~c~
  on the board writes, or ~how › steps~ -- and the head holds as its parts
  do.  Aspects, a 6W1H of what a law asks, say, check that the breakdown
  leaves nothing out.  What is still to find out is a question written
  here.
- /Make sense/, in the members.  Say what the facts mean here -- an
  interpretation -- and what is to be done -- a decision.  Each stands on
  the heads of the clusters it reads, through ~TRUSS_GROUNDS~, and on the
  interpretations it follows from; a decision says how it is done, in
  steps or parts nested under it.
- /Tell/, in the structure.  Sections, each a place in how the document
  is told, list the members the reader is shown, from either part.

So a fact belongs in the subject, and an interpretation among the members.
The board lists what is written in the other part as /misplaced/: a fact
among the members, or a question standing there on its own; a member of
the subject that stands on others rather than a source, or that a why
pattern argues for -- or that has members under it and no ~TRUSS_LOGIC~,
which reads as why.  ~f~ moves it; ~p~ gives a cluster of facts ~how ›
parts~.

The steps shape what the reader is given.  A member of the subject is
written out alone where a section lists it: its breakdown is how it was
found, and stays behind.  A member of the members is written out with what
is nested under it -- its reasons, its steps -- as it is argued.  A ground
is never written out; it is what the claim stands on.

A statement under the subject's constraints is a requirement, and what
answers it is seldom the statement itself.  A law says what must be done;
the document says what we do, and each thing it says it does stands on
the requirement it meets, through ~TRUSS_GROUNDS~.  So the board asks of a
constraint not whether it is placed but whether it is met: a cluster
nothing of which the document uses -- placed nowhere, and the ground of
nothing placed -- is listed as an /unmet constraint/.  The check is as fine
as the clusters are: a cluster is met as soon as anything in it is used.

A pattern is not a layer between arguments and structure.  A Warren truss is
a kind of truss, not a level between the truss and the building.

** What a member is

A member's kind follows from what is written on it.  Only two are declared.

| Kind       | How it is known                                                           |
|------------+---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| fact       | it has a ~TRUSS_SOURCE~                                                   |
| summary    | in the subject, a how pattern sums up the parts nested under it           |
| claim      | something supports it                                                     |
| hypothesis | a claim whose support, followed down, reaches an assumption or a question |
| assumption | tagged ~:assumption:~ -- taken as given, deliberately not proved          |
| question   | tagged ~:question:~ -- not known yet                                      |
| objection  | tagged ~:against:~, with no source or support of its own                  |
| step, part | nested under a member whose logic is how: says how, not why               |
| bare       | neither a source nor support: the board asks for one                      |

A member nested under another is what the other's ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ says, in
whatever part it is written: with a why pattern, or none, it supports it,
unless it is tagged ~:against:~, in which case it argues against it; with a
how pattern it is a step or a part of it.  A reply to an objection is tagged
~:against:~ too, since it argues against the objection.  Support that lives
elsewhere is pointed to with ~TRUSS_GROUNDS~, by links to the members it
names: ~[[#m3]] [[#m7]]~ (see [[*Pointing to a member]]).

The line between a fact and an assumption is whether you can say where it
came from.

** Why, and how

Nesting is the vertical of a pyramid.  Read upward, a member says what the
members under it come to -- /so what?/; read downward, they show that it
holds -- /why so?/.  The board checks the downward reading: a member with
neither support nor a source is bare.  Whether a claim says no more and no
less than what is under it is for you to read.

What is under a member answers the question it raises.  Most statements
raise /why?/, and what answers is support.  A statement of what to do
raises /how?/, and what answers it does not prove it; it spells it out.
So what is under a member answers one of the two, and its logic pattern
says which.

Across, the members under one are either joined or set side by side.
Joined, they hold it up only together, as a major and a minor premise do:
take one away and the rest prove nothing.  Side by side, each holds it up
on its own, and one more holds it up a little better; they are reasons, or
cases it generalises, or steps, or parts.

|     | joined    | side by side                                   |
|-----+-----------+------------------------------------------------|
| why | deduction | reasons, induction (cases)                     |
| how |           | steps (in time), parts (by what it is made of) |

A step or a part is not asked for support, and is not the member's
support either: why it is to be done is still owed, by ~TRUSS_GROUNDS~ or
from the member above.  It can have support of its own nested under it,
or steps or parts of its own.  What argues against the member is still
tagged ~:against:~, and is neither.

Whether a set of members leaves nothing out and says nothing twice -- is
MECE -- is for you to read, as so what is.  It can be checked in part when
the set is cut along a known line, its /aspects/: pros and cons; past,
present and future; who does it, to whom, when, where, what, why and how.
Each member covers the aspect its ~TRUSS_ROLE~ names, and the board lists
an aspect none covers, as it lists a deduction's missing premise, and a
member that covers none.  A document is written for someone, and what it
asks done is mostly done by someone to someone, so 6W1H is the cut to
reach for when nothing smaller does; 5W1H, without /whom/, is there for
what is done to no one.  A notice reads naturally cut this way, with its
steps inside its /how/:

#+begin_example
Check before every test

- Who: the driver's manager
- Whom: whoever drives in the test
- When: before the first run of the day
- How: as follows

  1. Meet the driver, face to face or by video call
  2. Read the checker
#+end_example

** Clusters

A heading with headings right under it is a cluster: a claim with its
support, a decision with its steps, a fact with the facts it sums up, a
section with its sections.  The structure is a cluster too, the largest:
its sections are the parts of the whole document.  Clusters hold clusters,
and each is read and checked alone, by its own patterns.

A cluster's patterns say what is right under it is to it, and so what it
asks for: a deduction asks for its two premises, aspects for each of
theirs, a telling pattern for each of its places.  Any cluster takes any
pattern, in any part.  Nothing is read into a cluster
from the part it is written in: with no ~TRUSS_LOGIC~, what is under a
member supports it, everywhere, and a section asks for nothing.  What
differs by part is only the pattern the board writes when a member starts
a cluster -- when ~c~ or ~f~ puts the first member under it: ~parts~ in the
subject and the brief, ~reasons~ in the members.  A new file's structure
starts with none, as there may be no document to make yet; ~p~ gives it
one when there is.  So what a cluster is can always be read in the file,
and changed there or with ~p~.

What a cluster asks for is filled by what is right under it, each by its
~TRUSS_ROLE~, and the board lists what is not filled as a /gap/, under the
cluster's line and under /Pattern gaps/.  Deeper down a role is filled for
that cluster, not this one: the situation inside a remedy is the remedy's.

** Members have no roles of their own

The same member can be the sky in one document and the grounds in
another.  Its place belongs to the cluster it is under, not to the member,
which is why a section's place lives on the section.

So ~TRUSS_ROLE~ says where a heading stands in the patterns of the one
above: a member nested under a deduction is its major or its minor
premise, a part of a 6W1H says which of the seven it covers, a section
under a Sky, Rain, Umbrella which of the three it is.  A heading fills one
place for each pattern that asks -- a major premise told as a reason is
~major reason~ -- for a section as for a member.

** Three kinds of pattern

A cluster's patterns are of three kinds, each answering its own question
and written as its own property, so each can be chosen without the others.

| Kind    | Property        | Says                               | Checked                                          |
|---------+-----------------+------------------------------------+--------------------------------------------------|
| logic   | ~TRUSS_LOGIC~   | how what is under it joins it      | each member's kind and support; premises, cases  |
| aspects | ~TRUSS_ASPECTS~ | what must all be under it          | an aspect none covers; a member that covers none |
| telling | ~TRUSS_TELLING~ | the order it is told in, by places | a place none fills; a section that fits no place |

The logic pattern is the truss itself: how its members are joined.  Why
answers with support -- reasons that each hold it up alone, the two
premises of a deduction held together, the cases an induction generalises
-- and how with what it is made of, steps in order or parts in none.
These five are the joints every argument is made of; a premise that needs
proving is itself the head of a deduction nested under it, so a chain of
reasoning is a tree of them.

Aspects are a breakdown that can be checked: a set of what must be there,
so that nothing is left out -- MECE's collectively exhaustive half -- and
each member under the cluster covers one of them, the other half.  They go
with reasons, steps and parts, the joints that are sets; a deduction's
premises and an induction's cases are what they are by their places, and
take none.  A member covering an aspect is written out labelled with it --
/Pros:/, /Who:/ -- whatever the logic.  On the brief and the subject the
aspects are their fields, and nothing there is written out; the brief and
the subject take aspects alone, since nothing of them is argued or told.

A telling pattern is a framework of telling, which says nothing of what is
told: its places in order.  On a member, the place that is its point --
PREP's point, SCQA's answer, the umbrella -- is the member itself, the
claim at the head of the pyramid, and the rest are filled by what is under
it; PREP and SDS tell the point again at the end.  A section says nothing
itself, so on a section every place is a section.

What fills the places is told in the order of the file, as everything in
the document is: the order the headings are written in is the order the
reader meets them, and the board shows it so.  What the file has below a
heading the pattern tells after it is marked =⚠= and listed /Out of
order/, to be moved in the file.  The one thing a pattern does that the
file cannot is put a member after what is nested under it, since an
outline always has a heading above what is under it: /conclusion first/
tells a member and then what is under it, the pyramid read top down, as
PREP, SDS and a member with no telling pattern do; /conclusion last/,
SCQA and Sky, Rain, Umbrella tell it after.

Where PREP or SDS tells the point again, the words can differ: a member's
=#+begin_opening= block says what it is told in where it opens, and its
=#+begin_closing= block where it closes.  Each, when it is not written,
is the public block -- or the words the section listing the member tells
it in, or its heading -- so with neither, the point is told twice in the
same words.  A conclusion told last is told in its closing block, one
told first in its opening block, when it has one.

Steps and parts differ by whether order matters.  /Keep a record of each
check/, in parts: the name of whoever checked, the driver, the time, the
reading, what was said; in any order, nothing is lost.  /Check before
every test/, in steps: meet the driver, then read the checker; in another
order, it is another procedure.

Sky, Rain, Umbrella is fact, interpretation, action: the sky is clouding
over; it will rain, then; take an umbrella.  It is a way of thinking as
much as of telling, and it tells the umbrella last.

Patterns nest: the umbrella of one Sky, Rain, Umbrella can hold another,
and a deduction can stand where SCQA puts its answer.

~p~ on the board offers every pattern the cluster can take, of each kind,
and takes one of a kind off.  What the pattern asks for and is not there
is put in, a heading for each place named for it, to be written over: a
deduction's premises, a PREP's reason and example, a 6W1H's seven.  On a
section, a telling pattern first takes the sections there into its places
by their roles, and puts them in its order in the file.  ~o~ tells a
member last, or one told last first.

Premises are asked of the structure as soon as an assumption is in use in
a document, whatever its patterns: a reader is owed what the argument
takes for granted.

~org-truss-patterns~ takes patterns of your own, of any kind -- a set of
aspects from ~org-truss-roles~, a framework of telling -- and
~org-truss-roles~ the places they name.

* The file

There is one ~truss.org~ per document, in a directory of its own.
~M-x org-truss-init~ sets the directory up; run on one set up before, it
adds what is missing and leaves what is there.

| ~truss.org~ | the manuscript; the rest is written from it or for it                  |
| ~sources/~  | what was read, a file for each source: a page, a PDF, an extract       |
| ~notes/~    | what is ours and not a source: memos, slides, minutes                  |
| ~attach/~   | what ~org-attach~ (~C-c C-a~) attaches to a heading: a picture, a file |
| ~out/~      | the document, written by ~x~ on the board or ~M-x org-truss-export~    |
| ~CLAUDE.md~ | for a coding agent: where the rules are, and when to read them         |

So the directory can be moved, or handed over, whole.  A fact's
~TRUSS_SOURCE~ names its file in ~sources/~ as well as where it came from.
The first line of
~truss.org~ sets ~org-attach-id-dir~ to ~attach/~, so what ~org-attach~ puts
on its headings is kept there, not where attachments go elsewhere, each
heading's in a directory named by its ~ID~; it is a file variable Org
declares safe, so it holds whether org-truss is loaded or not.  The board
and the document read a link to an attachment, ~[[attachment:NAME]]~, as a
link to its file.

~CLAUDE.md~ does not repeat the rules.  They are in the comment block at
the head of ~truss.org~, which an agent reads as it reads the file; the
agent file says only to read it before working there, to read a part's
~:GUIDE:~ before writing in it, and to run the check after.  So the rules
have one home, kept current by ~org-truss-refresh-guides~, and what is
loaded into every session stays a few lines.  ~org-truss-agent-file~ names
the file, or is ~nil~ for none.

A comment block at the head of ~truss.org~ holds the rules of the file, the same for
whoever edits it, by hand, on the board, or as a language model working in
the file: what each part holds, what a member and a cluster are, how one
member points to another, what is written out, what each property and tag
means, what goes in the directories beside it, and the command that checks
the file.  A folded ~:GUIDE:~ drawer
under each part and field says how to work there.  The board's keys are at
its foot, and what each check finds is said where it is reported.  Its headings are English whatever
~org-truss-language~ says: they name the parts and the fields, you do not
rewrite them, and the reader never sees them.

The guidance learns, and a file keeps the wording it was written with.
~M-x org-truss-refresh-guides~ puts the current wording back -- the comment
block and every drawer, adding those that were deleted -- and touches
nothing else you wrote.  A file from before fields were places, its fields
marked ~TRUSS_FIELD~, has them fill their places by ~TRUSS_ROLE~ instead,
and its brief and subject given the aspects a new file's have.

** Headings assert; the structure tells

A member's heading is the sentence it asserts, and what the checks read.
Links point to it by its ~CUSTOM_ID~, not its heading, so the heading can
be reworded at any time.  It can be wrong and can stand on others,
so write it to read on its own, pulled out of the document -- no "the
following", no "above", no "first".  The order it is told in is the
structure's, and a heading that leans on that order stops being true when
the structure changes.  What a heading asserts is tied to the reader's
question, in the brief: ask another question and the top claim changes,
while what it stood on stays.  It is not tied to how it is told.

The structure asserts nothing.  It chooses what the reader sees and in
what order, and writes only what guides them -- what is true of the
document rather than of the world, so it needs no grounds:

- a section's title, its heading;
- a section's lead, the text of its public block, read under the title
  before the members the section lists.  The structure's own lead opens
  the document;
- the words a member is told in there, as the description of the link
  that lists it.  The heading goes on asserting, plainly; the section says
  it as this reader needs, as a request in a notice, say.

#+begin_src org
,** What to do
,#+begin_public
The check runs as follows.
,#+end_public
,- [[#m12][Please have the check before and after you drive.]]
#+end_src

The joins between members are seldom the structure's to write either: a
member's pattern says how what is under it joins it -- steps numbered,
what covers an aspect labelled with it -- and the document is written so.  Whatever could be
wrong is a member, where the checks reach it.

A member told with the one above it -- a step, a part, a reason -- has no
link to carry its words.  Its public block says what the reader sees
instead of its heading, a title as well when it should have one:

#+begin_src org
,#+begin_public The title the reader sees
The text the reader sees.
,#+end_public
#+end_src

A link's words come first, then the member's public block, then its
heading.  Everything else under the heading -- why you believe it, who said it, what
cannot be said -- stays in the file.  A member tagged ~:noexport:~ stays in
the file whole, with what is nested under it: evidence you keep for
yourself, a note on what to check.  A document generated from the file can
be regenerated as often as you change it, so the file stays the place where
the thinking happens, and the document is never a copy that has to be kept
in step by hand.

** What the outline means

Each part is found by its ~TRUSS_PART~, at whatever level and in whatever
order the parts sit, so the file can be laid out as suits you.  Within the
parts, the outline carries meaning in these places only:

- Under /Members/, nesting is what the ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ of the member above
  says: support -- a member nested under another stands under it -- or,
  under a member whose logic is how, one of its steps or parts, and the
  order of steps is theirs.  The order of the members, and of the top ones
  especially, means nothing; the board and the document follow the links.
- Right under the /Brief/ or the /Subject/, a heading is a field: it fills
  a place of the part's aspects by its ~TRUSS_ROLE~, and its text says what
  fills it.
- Under a field of the /Brief/ or the /Subject/, a heading is a member that
  states the field, and nesting below it means what it means under
  /Members/: a cluster of facts says ~how › parts~.  The board never asks
  for one to be placed: a condition is there to be met, and shown only
  when you choose.
- Under /Structure/, the outline is the document's: its sections, in order,
  each answering for the cluster it stands right under.

So members can be reordered and their headings rewritten freely; grouping
them under a heading of their own, though, says that heading stands on them,
and a member moved under a section would be read as a section.  The board
does not depend on where in the file a member is written -- only on what it
is nested under and what links to it.

** Sections list members

A section lists the members it shows as link items, ~- [[#m7]]~.  The link
brings a member of the members with everything nested under it, and a
member of the subject alone.  Its description, if it
has one, ~- [[#m7][words]]~, is what the reader is told there in place of
the heading.  A section can hold sections of its own.

** Pointing to a member

A link in a section, and a ground in ~TRUSS_GROUNDS~, point to a member by
its ~CUSTOM_ID~: ~[[#m7]]~ is the member with ~:CUSTOM_ID: m7~.  Org
resolves such a link within the file alone -- ~C-c C-o~ follows it, in a
section or in a property -- and, unlike ~:ID:~, it is not entered in
~org-id-locations~, nor made a node by org-roam.

A heading is what a member asserts, and it gets reworded as the argument
and the document take shape.  Were links to name headings, each rewording
would break what pointed to it; pointing by id, a heading can be rewritten
anywhere -- on the board with ~R~, in the file by hand, or by a model
editing the file -- and nothing breaks.

A member is given an id only once something points to it: ~a~ placing it,
~b~ making it a ground, ~c~ writing it in a section.  An id is ~m~ and the
next number free in the file; one you write yourself is kept.  Two members
with one id are reported as /Duplicate ids/; a link or a ground that points
to no member, as /Broken links/ or /Broken grounds/.  ~a~ on a broken link
places a member where it stands.

A file written when links named headings -- ~[[*heading]]~, and headings
separated by ~;~ in ~TRUSS_GROUNDS~ -- shows them all as broken.
~M-x org-truss-convert-references~ points each by id, giving the member
named one, and keeps a link's words.  It does the same for a link written
by heading, with Org's completion, say.

* The board

~M-x org-truss-board~ draws the nearest ~truss.org~: under its title and
brief, its subject, its members and its structure, in the order the work
is done, each followed by what is left to do there.

The board is where a truss file is worked from, as the agenda is for Org
files.  It can be called from anywhere: when no ~truss.org~ is near the
current buffer, or with a prefix argument, it asks for one as ~find-file~
asks for a file -- the file, its directory, or anything below it -- and
offers to start one in a directory that has none, making the directory if
it is new.  The file's own buffer can be closed whenever it is not wanted:
the board visits it again before its next command.  ~C-x C-s~, or whatever
saves a buffer, saves it from the board, and the title line says
/unsaved/ until then.

Each line has three columns.  On the left is what the board says the line
is: the questions a section answers, the place a field fills, the kind of
a member -- marked =x= when it argues against the one above -- and the
tree that joins them.  At the
end of the left, just before its heading, a member is named by its
~CUSTOM_ID~ as the file names it: =#m9= where it is written, in the
subject and the members, and =[[#m9]]= where a section's link or a ground
calls it.  So what the structure shows can be found where it is written,
and a member nested under one a section calls, which is not called
itself, has none.  In the
middle, from one column the same on every line, are the file's own words --
headings, labels, the title, the brief -- as written, with dots leading to
them.  A member's heading is in the face Org gives a heading of its level,
so it looks as it does in the file; the board colours it itself, so it does
not matter whether the file has been shown yet.  The headings of the parts are not among them: the structure's, like
the brief's and the members', names a part and never reaches the reader, so
its line says only /Structure/.  Words too long for the middle are cut
short, the whole of them in the tooltip, so the right keeps its column.
The left is as wide as it needs; the middle and the right share the rest
of the window evenly, and are drawn again when it is resized.  What a
section or a member holds starts two columns right of its word: the
members a section lists, the grounds of a member.  The sections under a
section, and the members nested under a member, branch off from the
column of its word.  The line of the tree starts right under the line
naming its pattern and runs down past whatever comes before the first
branch -- its grounds or the members it lists, and with entry text its
notes -- so each cluster is held by it as far down as it goes.  The members' tree is drawn in the face
~org-truss-branch~, faint by default, so where it meets the sections' tree
in the structure the two read apart.

The three columns say the same in every part.  The left is where a line
stands in the logic; the middle, what is asserted, so whether the logic
holds -- or, in the structure, whether the story does -- is read down it;
the right, what the reader is told, so whether it reads as prose is read
down that.  A member's right is the same where it is written and where a
section shows it: the words the section tells it in, its public block, or
its heading; a step with its number, a part with the aspect it covers.
In the subject and the members it is in the face ~org-truss-untold~ while
no section places the member, nor one it is nested under, and empty under
a member of the subject, whose parts the document never tells.  So a
cluster can be read through as prose beside its logic where it is
written.

The links run both ways where a member is written, as lines in the middle
under it, and the reader is told nothing on them.  A line marked =→= is
where it is used: a section that lists it, by the place it fills and its
heading, then a member it is a ground of, by its kind and its heading.  A
line marked =⬑= is a member it stands on through ~TRUSS_GROUNDS~, written
elsewhere, with its kind: the arrow points up to what it supports, as =x=
marks what argues against.  Either is cut to the middle, its whole heading
in the tooltip, and ~RET~ on it goes to what it names; ~=~ goes between a
member and its calls.  A member with no line marked =→= is not used.  A
place a member's pattern asks for and it lacks -- /Minor premise —/ --
follows what it holds.  So does a place the structure's or a section's
pattern asks for and no section right below fills -- /Premises —/ -- and
~s~ on it adds a section that fills it.

In the structure the document can be read down the right, and a member
brings with it only what the document tells: what is nested under a
member of the members, and nothing under a member of the subject; not its
uses, its grounds or its gaps.  A member PREP or SDS tells again has a
line /again/ after what is under it, with the words it closes with,
wherever it is drawn.  A member told after what is under it -- conclusion
last, SCQA's answer, the umbrella -- has its words there too, on a line
named for its place, and a line runs down the right to them from its own
line:

A section with no role is marked /no role/ in the warning face where the
patterns of the section above name places for it to fill; elsewhere its
line says /section/.

What it stands on and what it lacks are drawn where it is worked on,
among the members.

Each part is followed by what is left to do in it.  Under the subject:
/Unmet constraints/; facts with /No source/; its /Open questions/ and
/Assumptions/; /Pattern gaps/; members with /No aspect/ of the cluster
above; /Placeholders/; and what is /Misplaced/ there.  Under the
members: what is /Not placed/, or used /Only as grounds/; /Bare/ members;
their /Open questions/, /Assumptions/ and /Unanswered objections/;
/Pattern gaps/; /No aspect/; members /Out of order/; /Placeholders/; what
is /Misplaced/ there; and /Broken grounds/.  Under the structure: its /Pattern gaps/ -- the
structure, or a section, with the places its patterns ask for and no
section right below fills -- sections /Out of order/, /Placeholders/,
/Empty sections/, /Broken links/, and /Duplicate ids/.  The mouse on the
name of a check says what it finds.

A placeholder is a heading still called what its place is called -- the
name ~p~ gave a heading it put in for a place, /Reason/, /雨/ -- which the
reader would meet as it is.  An empty section has no text in its public
block, no member listed and no section below: the document would show its
title alone.  The checks only say so; what to do about either is written
in the file.

A member is checked when the document uses it: placed in a section, or
the ground of one placed.  Until the structure tells anything -- a
pattern, a section, a link -- there is no document, and every member is
checked as if used, and none is /Not placed/.  So a file can hold a
question being worked out, with a colleague or with Claude, as facts,
assumptions, open questions and decisions, each checked as it is written,
and become a document only when one is wanted: ~p~ on the /Structure/
line gives it a telling pattern and its sections, or ~s~ a section.  A new
file's structure tells nothing.

The patterns a cluster follows go on the line just below it, in
parentheses, where the sections or members they arrange branch off: a section's
logic and telling patterns, as ~p~ offers them; a member's logic pattern
and where it is told.  A member with members under it always has the
line, so what they are to it shows even where no ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ is written
-- /why › reasons/, whose tooltip says it is not written.  A step or a part shows on the right the number, the
bullet or the label the reader will see it with, and a reason the label
of the aspect it covers.  A section marked =⚠= does not fit its parent's
pattern: none of the pattern's places asks its questions, or more than one
does -- or it comes below a section the pattern tells after it.  The
document gives sections in the order of the file, so the order is put
right there.  The mark's tooltip, or ~C-h .~ on the line, says which.

What the reader sees is the words the section tells a member in, when it
has some; else the title of the line's public block, or the start of the
block's text when it has no title, or else its heading -- for a section,
its heading in the document; /noexport/ when the reader sees nothing of it.
A placeholder is drawn in the warning face, since the reader would meet
the pattern's word for its place rather than words of its own: a section's
title on the right, a member's heading in the middle; its tooltip says
so.  On the /Structure/ line is the structure's own lead.  What becomes a heading in the document -- a section's title,
a member's public title -- is drawn as one; what becomes body text, in the
faint face the notes in the middle take.  The brief is the reader's only
through the title.  Under the title come the brief, then the subject,
each drawn as the structure is: its line, the pattern it follows, and its
fields as a tree, each named by the place it fills and followed by its
text, so what the structure has to meet stays in view.  A place the
pattern asks for and no field fills -- /Constraints —/ -- follows them,
and ~s~ on it puts in a field that fills it, where the pattern lists it;
the subject's are listed under /Pattern gaps/ too.  A field that fills
none of the pattern's places is named in the warning face, and ~r~ gives
it one.  The members written under a field are drawn below it, cluster
by cluster, and are gone to and placed from there.  An
assumption with a ~TRUSS_CONFIRM~ adds whom to confirm it with.  Whether a
member has a source needs no mark: that is what makes it a /fact/.

~E~, as in the agenda, shows what is written under each heading, below its
line: the notes that stay in the file in the middle, under the heading, and
the rest of what the reader sees on the right, broken to fit the window.
A heading too long for the middle is broken there too instead of cut
short, since the board is now for reading, and so is every other line: a
field's text, a ground, what a check lists.  The line naming a cluster's
pattern stays right under its line, on the left of what follows it; when
the pattern is too wide for the left it takes the line after the heading's
words, and the notes start below it.  Read down the right, the board is
then the document, near enough, beside the tree and the notes it was
built from:

~w~ does the same for the line at point alone, so the board can be scanned
a line each and one line read through: a member with its notes, a ground,
a field.  ~w~ again, on any of its lines, puts it back on one.  While ~E~
shows every line whole, ~w~ puts the line at point on one line instead,
and ~E~ again draws every line the same way.

A picture linked on a line of its own -- an attachment, or a file by a
relative path -- is drawn on a line drawn whole, in its column: as wide as
the column and no taller than ~org-truss-board-image-height~ lines, on the
lines it takes, so the tree beside it runs on down and the reader's column
stays where it is.  On one line, and where Emacs shows no pictures, a link
to a file reads as the file's name.

The board's words are English whatever ~org-truss-language~ says, so in a
file written in another language the two sides read apart at a glance.

The board is read-only.  Its commands change the truss file and draw the
board again.  The writing itself happens in the file: ~RET~, ~TAB~ and ~SPC~
open it where the line points, as they do in the agenda, and ~e~ opens just
that heading's entry beside the board, the rest of the file out of the way.
The entry they go to is shown with its blocks open -- its public block, its
quotation -- even where ~org-hide-block-startup~ folds blocks, since they
are part of what it says; the headings under it are left as they were.
As in a capture buffer, ~C-c C-c~ there saves the file and closes the
entry, and ~C-c C-k~ puts back what it held and closes it; its header line
says so.  ~C-c C-c~ is taken for this, so a source block in an entry is run
with ~C-c C-v e~ instead.
Back on the board after the file has changed, it draws again, and before
each command it draws again first if the file's text has changed, so the
command acts on the file as it is.  Only the text counts: fontifying the
file, which happens whenever it is shown, changes nothing the board holds.
An edit made while a command is asking something makes the command stop
and draw again rather than act on positions that have moved.

The keys fall into four groups, by what they work on.  /Write/ works on
the members themselves, the headings in the file.  /Place/ works on the
structure's links to them: a section lists the members it shows, and the
members do not move.  /Shape/ works on the sections, on what the structure
says itself, and on the patterns of sections and members.  /See/ moves about the board and the document.  The
foot of the board lists them in the same four lines.

A member is placed in one section at most.  ~a~ on a member placed
already moves it, and on a section offers first the members placed
nowhere.  On a broken link, ~a~ places the chosen member where the link
stands, in its place: the way to mend one.  ~d~ loses nothing written: a member taken out stays in the file,
listed as not placed, and a section taken out hands what it places up,
asking before its own title or notes are lost.  To be rid of a member,
~D~ deletes it, with what is under it and what placed it; a ground that
named it is left, a broken link on the board, so what stood on it shows.

~S~ writes what the structure says itself.  On a member a section lists,
it asks for the words the section tells it in, starting from the heading;
blank words, or the heading's own, take them off.  On a member told with
the one above it, which no link lists, and on a section, it opens the
public block beside the board as ~e~ opens an entry -- a section's lead, a
step's words -- writing an empty one first if there is none; a block left
empty is taken away again when the entry is finished.  On the /Structure/
line, it writes the document's opening.

~=~ goes between where a member is written and where it is called.  On a
line calling it -- a section's link, =[[#m9]]=, or a ground -- or showing
it under a member a section calls, it goes to where it is written, =#m9=,
unfolding what hides it.  There, it goes to the next call after the one
come from, and round, saying which of how many it is.  The line at point
is lit, and so are the other lines naming the member on it -- where it is
written, where it is called -- in the face ~org-truss-same~; set
~org-truss-board-highlight~ to nil for neither.

~z~ folds what is under a line into the line, which then says how many
it hides: a field's members, or what is nested under a member.  On a line
with nothing under it, ~z~ folds what it is under, so anywhere among a
field's members it folds the field.  A field holding more than
~org-truss-board-fold-limit~ members, five unless set, starts folded, so
the conditions at the top of the board stay short as they fill.

A fact keeps the words of its source with it, in a ~#+begin_quote~ block in
its own text, which stays in the file as notes do.  Copy the passage, in
Emacs or in a browser, and ~Q~ on the fact puts it there, or, after
asking, in place of the quotation already there.

A ground supports a member from outside what is nested under it, through
~TRUSS_GROUNDS~.  ~b~ on a member offers its grounds first, and choosing
one takes it away; any other is added, and given a ~CUSTOM_ID~ when it has
none.  On a ground's own line, the one marked =⬑=, ~b~ takes that ground
away at once.

On the /Structure/ line, ~p~ gives the document a telling pattern -- Sky,
Rain, Umbrella, SCQA, PREP -- and with it its sections, or aspects its
sections must cover; on a section, the same for the sections below it.
On the /Brief/ or the /Subject/ line, it offers aspects alone, and puts in
a field for each place they name that none fills.  ~no telling~, ~no
aspects~ and ~no logic~ take each off, and nothing is asked of what is
below for it.

The foot of the board lists the keys as they are bound where it is drawn,
so a key moved -- by an Evil configuration, say -- shows as it is.

** Writing members from the board

A statement can be started without deciding where it goes.  ~c~ asks for
its heading, writes it, and opens it as ~e~ does, below the heading, to be
written; ~C-c C-k~ there takes it away again.  Where it goes follows the
line at point:

- on a step or a part, just after it, as the next one -- on a labelled
  part, asking which role the next fills; in the subject, on a fact of a
  cluster, the next fact of that cluster;
- on any other member, under it, as its ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ says: support for
  it, or, when it says how to do something, its last step or part; on a
  gap, a role its patterns ask for that nothing fills, in that role.  A
  member with nothing under it yet starts a cluster, and ~c~ writes its
  ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ first, the usual one for its part: ~parts~ in the subject,
  so it becomes the head of a cluster of facts, ~reasons~ in the members;
- on a field of the brief or the subject, under the field -- on the
  brief's or the subject's own line it asks for a field instead;
- on a section, at the top of the members, and listed in the section;
- anywhere else, at the top of the members, where order means nothing.
  The board lists it as not placed until something uses it.

With a prefix argument, ~c~ on a member writes under it whatever it is:
support for one step, say.  A step that has steps of its own takes them as
any member that says how does, so on it ~c~ writes its last.

What a member is follows from a property or a tag, and those are hard to
think of while writing the statement itself.  The board says when a member
is /bare/, and ~t~ on it says what it is instead: a /fact/ asks where it
comes from and writes it as ~TRUSS_SOURCE~; an /assumption/ asks whom to
confirm it with and writes ~TRUSS_CONFIRM~; a /question/ is tagged; a
/claim/ stands on what is nested under it.  What would make it another
kind is taken off, except that a source stays on an assumption or a
question, as a note of where it was thought to come from.

~f~ refiles a member, as Org's ~C-c C-w~ refiles a heading: it moves it,
with what is nested under it, to the top of the members, under a field of
the brief or the subject, or under another member as what its
~TRUSS_LOGIC~ says -- support, a step, a part.  A member with nothing under
it is given the usual ~TRUSS_LOGIC~ for its part first, as ~c~ gives it.
Links and grounds point to it by its ~CUSTOM_ID~, which goes with it, so
the structure still shows it wherever it goes.  The places are offered in the order of the file, and a
member by its path -- from Members or its field, through what it is nested
under, to its own heading, with a step's number or a part's role:
/Members › Check before every … › step 2: Read the checker/.  ~a~ on a
section offers members the same way.

Applying a pattern to a section that already has sections puts each, as it
is, in the place of the new section it shares a question with; the pattern
adds sections, named in ~org-truss-language~, only for the places none
fills.  So switching patterns changes the order and the marks, never a
heading.  A section that shares no question, or questions with more than
one place, is kept after the rest, and the board says which.  ~a~, ~r~ and
~d~ sort it out.  ~u~ and ~d~ ask before discarding a title or notes.
~u~ takes the section's patterns off too, of every kind, since what they
asked for was asked of the sections it takes away.

* The document

~P~ on the board shows the document in another window without writing it,
and the board keeps it current while it is shown: the place to read it.
The right of the board comes close but is not the same.  The fields under
the title and the lists at the foot are not in the document; a conclusion
that comes last is drawn above its support but written below it; and
without ~E~ each line shows only a title or the start of a paragraph.

~M-x org-truss-export~, or ~x~ on the board, walks the structure and writes
what the reader sees to ~out/truss.org~ (~org-truss-output-directory~), then runs
pandoc, when it is installed, to make ~out/truss.md~.

- The structure's lead, its public block's text, opens the document.
- A section is titled by its public block, or failing that by its heading;
  the block's text, its lead, follows the title.
- A member is written in the words of the link that lists it, when it has
  some; else as its public block, or failing that as its heading, a
  paragraph.  One tagged ~:noexport:~ is left out, with what is nested under
  it.
- A titled member becomes a heading under its section.  When its conclusion
  comes last, its support comes first and its title opens its paragraph in
  bold, since a heading after its own support would leave the support under
  the heading before.
- What says how to do a member is written as a list: steps numbered, parts
  bulleted, labelled parts opening with their role, in the order of the
  roles.  What is nested under one goes inside its item, how to do it as a
  list again and the rest as paragraphs, titles in bold.  What argues
  against the member follows the list.
- A member told PREP comes first, then its reasons, its examples and the
  rest of its support, and then once more, as a paragraph: its public
  text, or its heading.
- A link to no member is written as /(broken link: …)/, where it will be seen.
- A link to a file by a relative path -- a picture in ~attach/~, a source --
  is written relative to ~out/~, so the document still finds it, and pandoc
  makes a picture an image.  ~P~ shows the pictures where they are.

pandoc is given ~org+east_asian_line_breaks~, so a filled paragraph of
Chinese, Japanese or Korean does not gain a space at every line break.
~org-truss-pandoc-arguments~ and ~org-truss-output-extension~ change the
target; pandoc's ~--reference-doc~ gives a slide or word-processor template.

The language is that of what org-truss writes for you to read or to
rewrite: a new file's guidance, the sections a pattern adds, and what the
document says of a broken link.  The board stays English.  Identifiers in
the file -- property values and tags -- stay in English too, and so do the
headings of a new file's parts and fields, so a file reads the same to
everyone.

* Batch

#+begin_src sh
emacs --batch -L . -l org-truss -f org-truss-batch-check  truss.org
emacs --batch -L . -l org-truss -f org-truss-batch-board  truss.org
emacs --batch -L . -l org-truss -f org-truss-batch-export truss.org
#+end_src

The first prints what is left to do, as ~M-x org-truss-check~ lists it:
under the name of each check that finds something and what it finds, a
line for each thing, ~truss.org:42: …~.  Anything that edits the file
without the board -- you by hand, or a model working in the file -- can
run it to see what it broke or left undone.  The comment block at the head
of a truss file gives this line, with the directory org-truss is loaded
from.  The second prints the board as text, for anything that reads the
structure without Emacs open.
